# Six Thinking Hats: Worked Examples

---

## Example 1: Business Decision — "Should we launch a mobile app?"

**Topic:** A mid-size e-commerce company is deciding whether to build a dedicated mobile app.

---

### 🔵 Blue Hat (Opening)
We're exploring whether to invest in building a native mobile app. We'll look at what we know,
how we feel, what could go wrong, what the upside is, what alternatives exist, and then decide
on next steps.

---

### 🤍 White Hat
- 65% of our web traffic comes from mobile devices
- Our mobile conversion rate (1.8%) is less than half our desktop rate (4.2%)
- Competitor A launched an app 18 months ago; Competitor B has not
- A basic app would cost ~$150k to build; estimated 9-month timeline
- App store average retention: 25% after 30 days
- We have no in-house iOS/Android expertise currently

---

### ❤️ Red Hat
- The dev team is excited — they want to build something native
- Customer service team is nervous: "more platforms = more support burden"
- CEO is enthusiastic but the CFO is wary after the last digital project overrun
- Gut feeling from the product team: customers want this, but mostly for repeat purchases

---

### 🖤 Black Hat
- Apps require ongoing maintenance; this isn't a one-off cost
- App store dependency is a risk — Apple/Google can change terms or de-list
- Push notification fatigue could damage our brand if misused
- Our 9-month build estimate is likely optimistic without mobile expertise
- If the app underperforms, we still have to maintain it — "zombie app" risk
- Smaller customer segments (B2B, older demographics) won't benefit at all
- We'd be competing on distribution with established players who have better app store SEO

---

### 💛 Yellow Hat
- A native app enables features that drive loyalty: wishlists, quick reorder, push discounts
- We could dramatically improve checkout conversion on mobile — even +1% = significant revenue
- App reviews and App Store presence give us a new discovery channel
- A well-designed app signals brand investment and modernity to customers
- We could capture first-party data more richly through app behaviour analytics
- Personalisation at scale becomes much more achievable

---

### 💚 Green Hat
- What if instead of building from scratch, we white-label an existing mobile commerce platform?
- What if we first do a Progressive Web App (PWA) to test demand before committing to native?
- What if we partner with a mobile dev agency and retain one in-house developer to own it long-term?
- What if the app's main value prop is NOT browsing but loyalty/rewards — a fundamentally different use case?
- What if we let our top 1,000 customers beta test a prototype before committing to full build?

---

### 🔵 Blue Hat (Synthesis)
The data is clear: our mobile experience is underperforming. The question is how to fix it.
The Black Hat surfaced real concerns about build cost, timeline, and ongoing maintenance.
The Green Hat offered a smart bridge: a PWA or white-label first approach would let us
test demand with lower risk. The Yellow Hat's case for loyalty features is compelling and
distinct from just replicating the website.

**Recommended next steps:**
1. Commission a PWA audit — can we close half the conversion gap without a full native app? (2 weeks)
2. Get two agency quotes for a phased native app build with loyalty-first positioning (1 month)
3. Survey top 500 customers on app appetite and preferred features (2 weeks)
4. Decision gate in 6 weeks with data

## Example 2: Personal Decision — "Should I quit my job to freelance?"

**Topic:** Someone is considering leaving full-time employment to go freelance.

---

### 🤍 White Hat
- Current salary: £52,000 + pension + 25 days holiday
- Freelance day rate in market: £300–450/day (industry average)
- To match current income, need ~180 billable days/year
- Have £12k savings (approx 4 months runway)
- No freelance clients yet, but 3 former colleagues have expressed interest
- Local market has moderate demand for my specialism

---

### ❤️ Red Hat
- Feel stuck and underchallenged in current role
- Excited by the idea of choosing my own clients and projects
- Fear: what if I can't find work?
- Part of me worries I'm romanticising freedom and underestimating the isolation

---

### 🖤 Black Hat
- No guaranteed income — income variability is real and stressful
- Lose employer pension contributions, healthcare benefits, and annual leave
- First 3–6 months may produce little income while building pipeline
- Self-employment requires admin (tax, invoicing, contracts) that takes time
- Freelancing can be isolating — no colleagues, no team energy
- Returning to employment after 2+ years freelancing may be harder

---

### 💛 Yellow Hat
- Potential to earn significantly more than salary once established
- Freedom to choose projects aligned with values and skills
- Flexibility in working hours and location
- Build a diverse portfolio across industries — more resilient long-term career
- The 3 warm leads could become anchor clients quickly

---

### 💚 Green Hat
- What if I negotiated a 4-day week at current employer first to test freelancing one day/week?
- What if I built up two freelance clients while still employed, then resigned?
- What if I freelanced for 12 months with a clear "return to employment" clause in my mental contract?
- What if I found a freelance co-working community to address the isolation concern?
- What if I reframed this as a 2-year experiment, not a permanent decision?

---

### 🔵 Blue Hat (Synthesis)
The case for going immediately is weakened by having no existing clients and only 4 months
runway. The emotional case is real but the fear in the Red Hat is also real. The Green Hat
idea of building clients while still employed directly addresses the biggest Black Hat risk.

**Suggested approach:** Set a 90-day goal to secure one recurring freelance client while
still employed. If achieved, give notice. If not, re-evaluate. This transforms a binary
decision into a staged, lower-risk transition.

## Example 3: Team Conflict — "Two team members have very different working styles"

**Topic:** A team lead is dealing with conflict between a "move fast" engineer and a
"process-heavy" architect. The team is slowed down by their disagreements.

---

### 🤍 White Hat
- Last sprint: 3 of 8 tickets delayed due to design disagreements
- Engineer (Alex): ships fast, prefers to refactor later, high output volume
- Architect (Bara): insists on thorough design review before coding, rarely misses edge cases
- Both have strong performance records individually
- Two features have been redesigned post-launch (both Alex's work) in last quarter
- No formal design review process exists currently

---

### ❤️ Red Hat
- Alex feels micromanaged and blocked
- Bara feels their concerns are dismissed as "slowing things down"
- Team morale is affected — others avoid getting caught between them
- The team lead feels exhausted by being the tie-breaker

---

### 🖤 Black Hat
- Without structure, this dynamic will continue or escalate
- If Alex leaves, velocity drops; if Bara leaves, technical debt grows
- Choosing sides will damage the relationship with the other person
- A poorly designed process could slow things even more

---

### 💛 Yellow Hat
- Both working styles have genuine value — the team has both speed and quality
- This is a solvable structural problem, not a personality problem
- Resolving it could make the team genuinely excellent rather than choosing one extreme
- The conflict is surfacing a real gap: no agreed design review criteria

---

### 💚 Green Hat
- Create a "design threshold" rubric: small changes skip review, large/risky ones require it
- Run a team retrospective where Alex and Bara present the value of their respective approaches
- Pair Alex and Bara on one feature deliberately — structured collaboration may build mutual respect
- Introduce async design docs (lightweight, time-boxed) to reduce live conflict
- Let the team vote on a process — shared ownership reduces "it's being imposed on me" resistance

---

### 🔵 Blue Hat (Synthesis)
The core issue is structural: no agreed process means every decision becomes a negotiation
between two personalities. The Green Hat solution — a design threshold rubric — directly
addresses this. Run a short workshop with Alex and Bara to co-create the criteria.
They'll both be more likely to follow a process they helped design.

**Next steps:** Schedule a 1-hour "design process" workshop with both, facilitated by you.
Outcome: a one-page decision framework agreed by both. Follow up in 30 days to assess.

## Example 4: Technical decision — "Do we adopt a new primary database?"

**Topic:** Engineering leadership is debating whether to migrate from the current managed
SQL offering to a different engine (or self-hosted) for cost and performance reasons.

### 🔵 Blue Hat (Opening)
We are exploring a potential database platform change. We will separate facts, instinct,
risks, benefits, and creative migration paths before any recommendation.

### 🤍 White Hat
- Current DB handles ~12k QPS peak; p95 read latency is 45 ms
- Storage growth is ~8% per quarter; backup/restore drills pass on schedule
- Vendor has announced a price step at next renewal in ~9 months
- Team has strong operational runbooks for the current stack; limited experience with the proposed engine
- No production workload has been run on the new option yet — only benchmarks and a spike doc

### ❤️ Red Hat
- Staff engineers feel fatigue: "another migration proposal"
- Hiring market suggests new graduates know the proposed stack well — recruiting may get easier
- On-call rotation is anxious about being first to debug unfamiliar failure modes

### 🖤 Black Hat
- Cutover windows and dual-writes are classic failure points; partial migration is hard to reason about
- Hidden coupling: reporting jobs, BI tools, and legacy services may not be migration-ready
- Cost savings may evaporate if we need extra SRE headcount or premium support
- Rollback after schema divergence may be impractical

### 💛 Yellow Hat
- If benchmarks hold in production, we could retire several workarounds and hot indexes
- Licensing or committed spend changes could materially improve unit economics
- A well-scoped migration can force documentation and remove years of accidental complexity
- Better alignment with a strategic cloud vendor discount or enterprise agreement

### 💚 Green Hat
- Run a read-replica or shadow-traffic phase before any write path moves
- Migrate only one bounded domain first (e.g., audit logs) as a learning vehicle
- Negotiate renewal with incumbent while benchmarking — use data from both paths
- Consider managed offerings of the new engine first; defer self-hosting until margins prove it

### 🔵 Blue Hat (Synthesis)
The decision hinges on **evidence under real traffic** and **total cost of change**, not
benchmarks alone. The Black Hat rightly stresses cutover and coupling risk; the Green Hat
suggests bounded pilots that reduce blast radius.

**Recommended next steps:**
1. Produce a one-page "coupling inventory" (systems, jobs, access patterns) — owner + 2 weeks
2. Shadow-traffic or read-replica proof in non-prod then canary — success criteria written first
3. Financial model: migration + 18-month ops vs renewal scenario — finance review

## Tips for Using These Examples

These are model outputs to guide the format and depth of analysis. Real sessions will vary:
- Depth per hat should match the complexity of the topic
- Don't force every hat to have equal length — some topics have more facts, some more risk
- The Blue Hat synthesis is the most important output; make it specific and actionable
- For personal decisions, be especially attentive to the Red Hat — emotions often contain
  the real decision already made
