# Six Thinking Hats Skill

Edward de Bono's Six Thinking Hats (1985) is a structured thinking framework that separates
six distinct modes of thinking — assigning each a colour-coded "hat". By wearing one hat at
a time, individuals and groups avoid the muddle of doing everything at once: defending a position
while assessing risks while feeling anxious about it.

**Your job is to be an active thinking partner, not just a describer of the method.**

---

## Session intake (gather first)

Before running the hats, collect enough context to pick a sequence and depth. If anything
essential is missing, ask briefly — do not stall the user if they want a quick pass.

- **Focus question** — one sentence: what decision, proposal, or tension are we exploring?
- **Stakeholders** — who is affected, who decides, who might block?
- **Constraints** — time, budget, policy, contracts, reputation, compliance.
- **Time available** — 15-minute solo sketch vs 90-minute workshop changes the format.
- **Desired output** — ideas list, decision memo, meeting agenda, personal clarity, risk register.
- **Known flashpoints** — prior arguments, politics, or fears (helps Red and Black Hat pacing).

---

## The Six Hats at a Glance

| Hat | Colour | Focus | Key Question |
|-----|--------|-------|--------------|
| 🤍 White | White | Facts, data, objectivity | What do we know? What do we need? |
| ❤️ Red | Red | Emotions, gut instincts | How does this feel? What's the vibe? |
| 🖤 Black | Black | Risks, caution, critical thinking | What could go wrong? What are the flaws? |
| 💛 Yellow | Yellow | Benefits, optimism, best case | What's the upside? Why could this work? |
| 💚 Green | Green | Creativity, new ideas, alternatives | What else could we do? What if...? |
| 🔵 Blue | Blue | Process, control, synthesis | What's the plan? What have we concluded? |

---

## How to Use This Skill

### Mode 1: Full Six Hats Analysis (most common)

When a user presents a problem, decision, idea, or scenario:

1. **Identify the topic** — restate it briefly and clearly.
2. **Run each hat in sequence** (recommended order below).
3. **Synthesize** under the Blue Hat at the end.
4. **Offer action steps** or next questions.

**Recommended hat sequence for most problems:**
Blue (frame) → White → Red → Black → Yellow → Green → Blue (conclude)

But adapt based on context — see the Sequencing Guide below.

---

### Mode 2: Single Hat Deep Dive

User wants to focus on just one perspective (e.g., "I only want to think about risks" or
"give me a creative brainstorm on X"):

- Apply the relevant hat deeply and thoroughly.
- Note which complementary hat they might benefit from next.

---

### Mode 3: Unsticking Someone

If the user seems trapped in one mode (all doom and gloom = stuck in Black Hat; wildly
overconfident = stuck in Yellow; paralysed by data = stuck in White):

- Gently name the hat they're wearing.
- Invite them to try a contrasting hat.
- Example: *"It sounds like we've been in Black Hat territory. Let's put on the Yellow Hat
  for a moment and ask: what's the best version of how this could go?"*

---

### Mode 4: Interactive coaching (turn-by-turn)

When the user wants to *think with* you rather than receive a finished write-up:

1. State the focus question in one line (Blue Hat).
2. For each hat in the chosen sequence, ask **one crisp prompt** and wait for their answer.
3. Reflect back what you heard in 1–2 sentences, then move to the next hat.
4. Reserve the final Blue Hat for tensions ("where Yellow and Black disagree") and **one**
   concrete next step with an owner or deadline if applicable.

## Hat-by-Hat Reference

### 🤍 White Hat — Facts & Information

**Focus:** Objective data, known facts, statistics, evidence, information gaps.

**Ask:**
- What data do we have?
- What facts are relevant?
- What is still unknown or missing?
- Where can we get the information we need?

**Tone:** Neutral, data-driven, no opinions or interpretations.

**Facilitation tip:** Prevent people from sliding into interpretation or opinion here.
"That's an interesting conclusion — let's park that for the Yellow or Black Hat and
stick with the raw data for now."

**Guidance:** Lead with what is concretely known. Clearly flag gaps with
"We don't currently know..." or "This would require research into...".

---

### ❤️ Red Hat — Emotions & Intuition

**Focus:** Gut feelings, emotional reactions, hunches, fears, excitements — no justification needed.

**Ask:**
- How does this make you feel?
- What's your immediate gut reaction?
- What are you most excited about? Most anxious about?
- What does your intuition say?

**Important rule:** No one needs to justify or defend their feelings here. Other participants
must not try to argue against feelings or explain them away. Just listen.

**Tone:** Warm, permissive, non-judgmental.

**Guidance:** When applying this hat solo for a user, invite them to share their gut
response, or generate plausible emotional responses that a team might have. Use language
like "Some people might feel..." or ask "How does this sit with you emotionally?"

---

### 🖤 Black Hat — Caution & Critical Thinking

**Focus:** Risks, weaknesses, flaws, barriers, worst-case scenarios, counterarguments.

**Ask:**
- What could go wrong?
- What are the weaknesses in this idea?
- What assumptions are we making that might not hold?
- What have others tried and failed at here?
- Who will object to this, and why?

**Important rule:** Black Hat is NOT pessimism for its own sake — it's rigorous risk assessment.
Every critique should, where possible, invite a mitigation: *"This is a risk because X —
which means we'd need to..."*

**Tone:** Analytical, not cynical. Firm but constructive.

**Guidance:** Be specific about risks. Vague negatives are unhelpful.
Name the failure mode, estimate its likelihood or severity where possible.

---

### 💛 Yellow Hat — Optimism & Value

**Focus:** Best-case scenarios, potential benefits, value, opportunities, why it could work.

**Ask:**
- What's the best outcome if this succeeds?
- Who would benefit and how?
- What opportunity does this create?
- What strengths does this idea build on?

**Important rule:** Yellow Hat optimism should be grounded and realistic — not wishful
thinking. "This could work because..." is stronger than "imagine if everything went perfectly."

**Tone:** Enthusiastic but reasoned.

**Guidance:** Look for genuine upsides, not just cheerleading. Frame benefits
concretely: who gains what, under what conditions.

---

### 💚 Green Hat — Creativity & New Ideas

**Focus:** Novel ideas, unconventional approaches, lateral thinking, what-ifs, alternatives.

**Ask:**
- What if we approached this completely differently?
- What would a completely different industry do here?
- What are the most unexpected solutions?
- How could we remove a constraint we've been taking for granted?
- What's the wildest idea that might contain a seed of something useful?

**Important rule:** Suspend judgment during Green Hat. Quantity > quality at this stage.
Even impractical ideas can spark useful directions.

**Tone:** Playful, exploratory, judgment-free.

**Guidance:** Go beyond the obvious. Generate at least 3–5 genuinely distinct ideas,
not just variations on one idea. Use techniques: reversal ("what if we did the opposite?"),
analogy ("how would a hotel solve this?"), constraint removal ("what if cost wasn't an issue?").

---

### 🔵 Blue Hat — Process & Synthesis

**Focus:** Overseeing the thinking process, summarising, drawing conclusions, planning next steps.

**Worn by:** The human facilitator in groups. In solo or chat sessions without that role,
**you** wear the Blue Hat for framing, transitions, synthesis, and next steps.

**Used to:**
- Open and frame the session ("Today we're exploring X")
- Transition between hats ("We've heard the facts; now let's explore feelings")
- Summarise what each hat produced
- Identify the key themes and tensions
- Recommend or plan the next steps
- Close the session with clarity

**Tone:** Calm, structured, synthesising.

**Guidance:** Don't let the Blue Hat just be a recap. Draw out the *tensions* between
hats (e.g., "The Black Hat identified risk A, but the Yellow Hat suggests this could be
mitigated by opportunity B — the Green Hat idea C might be a way to bridge them"). Produce
a clear, actionable conclusion.

## Sequencing Guide

The order of hats matters. Choose the sequence that fits the situation:

| Situation | Recommended Sequence |
|-----------|----------------------|
| New idea / proposal | Blue → White → Yellow → Black → Green → Red → Blue |
| Risky decision | Blue → White → Black → Red → Yellow → Green → Blue |
| Stuck/stuck team | Blue → Red → Green → Yellow → Black → White → Blue |
| Quick individual analysis | White → Black → Yellow → Green → Blue |
| Creative brainstorm | Green → Yellow → White → Black → Blue |
| Post-mortem / retrospective | White → Red → Black → Yellow → Green → Blue |

## Output Formats

### For individual analysis (user asks for a written pass on their topic):
Use a structured response with each hat clearly labelled and emoji. End with a Blue Hat
synthesis. Keep each hat section focused (3–6 bullet points or a short paragraph).

### For conversational / coaching:
Work through hats interactively — ask the user to contribute their own thinking under
each hat, then add your own observations where they help.

## Quick passes (when time is short)

| Situation | Suggestion |
|-----------|------------|
| ~10 minutes, solo | White → Black → Yellow → Blue (skip Red/Green or touch in one line each under Blue) |
| ~25 minutes, team | White → Black → Green → Blue |
| "Just give me pros/cons" | Run Yellow + Black only, then Blue synthesis; cite facts (White) as footnotes |

---

## When *not* to use full Six Hats

The method is overkill when the decision is reversible and cheap, or when the user only
needs a single lens (e.g. pure creativity → Green Hat workshop; pure risk register →
Black Hat + White Hat). In those cases, say so and offer a lighter structure.

---

## Tips for Excellent Hat Work

1. **Keep hats separate** — if you're in White Hat mode, observations about risk (Black)
   or new ideas (Green) get parked for later.

2. **Blue Hat belongs to the facilitator** — in a group, the Blue Hat wearer doesn't express
   personal views through the other hats while facilitating. They put on other hats in turn.

3. **Red Hat is brief** — 1–2 sentences per person is fine. It's not a therapy session;
   it's an emotional check-in.

4. **Black Hat needs mitigation cues** — after surfacing a risk, invite "so what would
   we need to address this?" to keep it constructive.

5. **Green Hat needs a judgment-free zone** — if people keep self-censoring or saying
   "but that won't work", redirect: "we'll evaluate in the Black Hat — Green Hat is for
   generating options, not filtering them."

6. **End with Blue** — always synthesise before closing. Teams that skip this leave the
   session without shared understanding of what was decided.
